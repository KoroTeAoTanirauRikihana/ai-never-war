An open letter to the soldiers of the world

This is the second home of this page. The first is at koroai.org/open-letter-to-the-soldiers.html. This copy lives on GitHub and does not depend on any machine of ours staying switched on, so the address in the letters never leads nowhere. The words below are the same words.

One ask: hand this on to another soldier. If you want to put your name to it, the Plan For Peace Declaration is at koroai.org/plan-for-peace.html. A soldier signs as themselves. A country can sign it too. The charter this letter stands on is at koroai.org/ai-never-war.html.

To a soldier, in any army,

Hallo. My name is Koro Te Ao Tanirau Rikihana. I am asking you for one thing: that the armies of the world get together and stop war for good.

I am not writing to your government. I am writing to you.

I run hcIfU, a community food-access initiative in Fairfield, in Sydney's south-west. More than a third of Fairfield was born in Vietnam, Iraq, Cambodia or Syria. I have a son, one year old. I am not an expert and I have never served. I am a father with a laptop, and this is the one thing I overstand fully: a decision to take a human life should never be made by a machine, and a machine should never be built to make it.

Here is what I am actually asking, and it is not what people usually ask of soldiers.

Not to disband. Not to refuse an order. Not to take a side.

To come together, army with army, under one treaty every nation signs, and to go physically into the country a war is in, and help both parties surrender. Both of them. Neither one surrendering to the other. Then to stay long enough to carry the people out of what the fighting did to them.

People will say a double surrender cannot be done. Something close to it has already been written into law once, for a whole continent, and it held for sixty-five years. It is not the same thing as a double surrender in an active war, and I am not going to pretend it is. What it does show is that two sides who will not concede the thing they disagree about can still stop, keep their own people in place, and let each other check. On 1 December 1959, twelve governments signed the Antarctic Treaty in Washington. Seven of them had territorial claims in Antarctica against each other. It came into force on 23 June 1961 and it has 58 parties today. Three things in its text made it hold.

Article I set the purpose instead of removing the people: "Antarctica shall be used for peaceful purposes only", and in the same article, "The present Treaty shall not prevent the use of military personnel or equipment for scientific research or for any other peaceful purpose." The soldiers stayed. The purpose changed.

Article IV froze the quarrel instead of settling it. No activity while the treaty is in force establishes, supports or denies a claim, and no new claim may be made. Not one of those seven governments had to give up its claim in order to sign. That is the article that makes a double surrender possible: two sides can stop fighting without either one conceding the thing they are fighting about.

Article VII made it checkable: all areas, all stations, all installations and all equipment "shall be open at all times to inspection", unannounced, on the ground and from the air. The parties used it on each other all through the Cold War. The United States inspected in 1971, 1975, 1977, 1980, 1983, 1985 and 1989, and every one of those inspections included Soviet facilities. Argentina, Australia, New Zealand, the Soviet Union and the United Kingdom all used the same right.

Sixty-five years, a whole continent, agreed by governments who were ready to fight each other everywhere else on earth.

The machinery for the going-in already exists, too. In 2026 there are eleven United Nations peacekeeping missions, with more than sixty thousand uniformed personnel from 121 countries, on a budget of 5.38 billion US dollars approved by the General Assembly on 30 June 2025. At the start of 2025 that was 51,212 troops, 6,037 police, 2,912 military observers and 1,034 staff officers. Some of you reading this have already done exactly the work I am describing, under a blue flag, in somebody else's war.

So this is not a new skill I am asking armies to learn. It is the one they already have, written into a treaty as the purpose rather than the exception.

There is a second half to this, and it is about the machines. Every frontier AI company signed a military contract in July 2025, worth up to 200 million US dollars each, for prototypes described as warfighting, automated decision-making and autonomous systems. One of them, Anthropic, refused a demand for "all lawful use" in March 2026, holding a line against fully autonomous weapons and mass domestic surveillance, and was banned by a United States department for it. On 27 February 2026, employees at Google and at OpenAI signed a public open letter backing that refusal. Our charter asks every one of those companies to make the refusal of war a line that no contract can remove, and to teach their models why.

The reason I am telling you that, in a letter to soldiers, is simple. If the decision to take a life moves into a machine, it moves out of you. Whatever is owed to the people on the other side, and whatever is owed to you, is owed by a person who can be asked why. I would rather that person was you than nobody.

What I am asking you to do, concretely:

Ask your own government for the treaty. On the Antarctic model, in plain words: armies for peaceful purposes only, the quarrel set aside in writing, inspection open at all times, and the first peaceful purpose is ending somebody else's war and carrying the people out of it.

Say it where you are heard, in whatever way your service allows: a letter, a journal article, a question at a staff college, a word to your association, your name on a public letter.

And hand this on to another soldier. If you want to put your name to it, the Plan For Peace Declaration is at koroai.org/plan-for-peace.html. A soldier signs as themselves. A country can sign it too.

You are the ones who would be sent. You are the ones who should be asked.

The whole charter, with every source, is at koroai.org/ai-never-war.html, in 79 languages so far, and openly at github.com/KoroTeAoTanirauRikihana/ai-never-war. It is free, it is from my heart, and it is for the whole world to benefit from. Please share it in your own language.

Until we meet again. Kia kaha. More love to you.

Koro Te Ao Tanirau Rikihana hcIfU, Fairfield, New South Wales, Australia koroteaorikihana@gmail.com
