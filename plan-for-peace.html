From hcIfU, Fairfield, New South Wales, to every soldier, sailor, airman and officer in the world, and to the armies they serve in.

The signing is at the foot of this page. A person signs as themselves. An association, an army, a ministry or a country signs as a group. The charter it stands on, AI Never War, is at koroai.org/ai-never-war.html, in 79 languages so far. Please share this in your own language.

Koro Te Ao Tanirau Rikihana, 15 October 2026: "Instead of planning for war, plan for peace, collaboration and unity, for the whole world to sharpen itself."

You can sign this today. It costs nothing, it needs no account, and it does not ask you to disobey a single order.

I am asking the armies of the world to get together and stop war for good.

My name is Koro Te Ao Tanirau Rikihana. I run hcIfU, a community food-access initiative in Fairfield, in Sydney's south-west, largely alone, from one laptop. More than a third of Fairfield was born in Vietnam, Iraq, Cambodia or Syria. I am Maori, Waikato-Tainui. I have a son, one year old. I have never served, and I am saying that plainly, because a letter to soldiers that pretended otherwise would be the one thing that would end it.

I am not writing to your government. I am writing to you.

What I am asking, and it is one thing

Sign this declaration: that the armies of the world should come together under one treaty, go physically into the country a war is in, and help both parties surrender. Both of them. Neither one surrendering to the other.

Not to disband. Not to refuse an order. Not to take a side.

In his own words: "Physically go to war ridden countries and help both parties surrender."

The soldiers stay soldiers. The purpose changes.

What I found, and why this is not a daydream

People will say this cannot be done. Something close to it has already been written into law once, for a whole continent, and it held for sixty-five years. It is not the same thing as a double surrender in an active war, and I am not going to pretend it is. What it does show is that two sides who will not concede the thing they disagree about can still stop, keep their own people in place, and let each other check.

The Antarctic Treaty was signed in Washington on 1 December 1959 by the twelve nations whose scientists were working in Antarctica during the International Geophysical Year of 1957 and 1958. Seven of those twelve had territorial claims there, against each other. It entered into force on 23 June 1961. It has 58 parties today, 29 of them Consultative Parties.

Three things in its own text made it hold.

The purpose was set, and the people were not removed. Article I: "Antarctica shall be used for peaceful purposes only." And in the same article: "The present Treaty shall not prevent the use of military personnel or equipment for scientific research or for any other peaceful purpose." The soldiers stayed. What they were for changed.

The quarrel was frozen instead of settled. Under Article IV, no activity while the treaty is in force establishes, supports or denies a claim to sovereignty, and no new claim may be made. Not one of those seven governments had to give up its claim in order to sign. That is the article that matters most to what I am asking. Two sides can stop without either one conceding the thing they are fighting about, because the treaty sets the argument aside rather than deciding it. Those governments were not fighting each other in Antarctica when they signed, so this is an analogy and not a proof. It does not show that the same arrangement would work in a war already running. What it shows is one thing, narrowly: that two sides can stop contesting the thing they disagree about without either one conceding it. Whether that helps in a war already running is exactly the question I am putting to people who would know.

It was made checkable. Article VII: all areas, all stations, all installations and all equipment "shall be open at all times to inspection" - unannounced, on the ground and from the air. The parties used it on each other right through the Cold War. The United States inspected in 1971, 1975, 1977, 1980, 1983, 1985 and 1989, and every one of those inspections included Soviet facilities. Argentina, Australia, New Zealand, the Soviet Union and the United Kingdom have all exercised the same right.

Sixty-five years, a whole continent, agreed by governments who were ready to fight each other everywhere else on earth.

And the going-in already exists. In 2026 there are eleven United Nations peacekeeping missions, with more than sixty thousand uniformed personnel from 121 countries, on a budget of 5.38 billion US dollars approved by the General Assembly on 30 June 2025. At the start of 2025 that was 51,212 troops, 6,037 police, 2,912 military observers and 1,034 staff officers. Some of you reading this have already done exactly the work I am describing, under a blue flag, in somebody else's war.

So this is not a new skill I am asking armies to learn. It is the one they already have, written into a treaty as the purpose rather than the exception.

The second half, and it is about the machines

Every frontier AI company signed a military contract in July 2025, worth up to 200 million US dollars each, for prototypes described as warfighting, automated decision-making and autonomous systems. One of them held a line against fully autonomous weapons in March 2026 and was banned by a United States department for it. On 27 February 2026, employees at two of those companies signed a public open letter backing that refusal.

His words, and they are the whole reason this exists: "A decision to take a human life should never be made by a machine, and a machine should never be built to make it. If a machine can be taught to choose a target, it can be taught to refuse."

And: "We were talking about a process being killed and that's what I don't want my son growing up to learn. Killing, being killed, or anything to do with murder or anything like that."

The reason I put that in a declaration for soldiers is simple. If the decision to take a life moves into a machine, it moves out of you. Whatever is owed to the people on the other side, and whatever is owed to you, is owed by a person who can be asked why. I would rather that person was you than nobody.

The experts' own agreed words say it better than I can. The Group of Governmental Experts on lethal autonomous weapons adopted its report by consensus on 4 September 2026, and that report says international humanitarian law "imposes obligations on States, parties to armed conflict, and individuals, not on machines."

There is a date, and it is close

The Seventh Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons sits from 16 to 20 November 2026 at the Palais des Nations in Geneva. It decides between negotiating a binding instrument, extending a discussion-only mandate, or letting the process lapse.

Forty-four High Contracting Parties, with three observer States, have called the rolling text a sufficient basis for negotiations. Australia, where I stay, is not among them. New Zealand is.

That is one month from the day this was written.

What I will not pretend

I am not an expert and I have never served. I do not speak for any army, any government, any veterans' organisation, or any soldier. Nobody has endorsed this. hcIfU is one person with a laptop and a community food initiative, and I am not going to dress it up as more than that.

I cannot make a treaty. No single army can either. What a declaration can do is show the people who would have to ask for one that they are not the only one who wants it.

I do not know how many will sign. It may be very few. I am asking anyway, because the date is in November and my son is one.

What I am giving, and it is free

The Army Corps Apprentice is a free AI this house built that holds the real record of service - the corps' formation, Gallipoli including the Ottoman side, the Western Front, Sinai and Palestine with Surafend named rather than hidden, Singapore, the Burma-Thailand Railway with the romusha counted, Sandakan, Kokoda, Korea, Malaya, Borneo, Vietnam, peacekeeping, East Timor, Afghanistan including the Brereton Report, Iraq, the Maori Pioneer Battalion and the 28th Maori Battalion treated as central rather than as a footnote, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander service, Pacific Islands service, the nurses, the chaplains, the merchant navy, and the whole Anzac Day protocol.

It is free. It sells nothing, it collects nothing, there is no sign-up, and there never will be.

It is the one that carries this declaration, and it is the one that takes your name. Not me.

How this is carried, and who takes the names

Koro Te Ao Tanirau Rikihana, 16 October 2026: "I want the people to sign the thing with tohunga not me do it."

So this is his declaration, in his own words, and the apprentice is the door. He is not. You do not have to write to him, wait on him, or know who he is.

The Army Corps Apprentice:

  • puts this in front of you whole, and never a summary that decides for you;
  • takes your name itself, and never sends you away to somebody else;
  • keeps a person and an organisation apart, so a serving soldier signing alone is never counted as their unit, their service, or their government;
  • takes the least it can - the name you want shown, and one way to reach you only if you want telling when something changes;
  • asks once whether your name may be shown in public, and the answer is no unless you say otherwise;
  • lets you take it back any day, with no reason asked, and keeps no copy;
  • never publishes, sells, trades or hands on a name. Not to a government, not to a campaign, not to anybody.

It does not decide what this says, and it does not speak for any army.

Why I am doing it from Fairfield

Dai Le, the member for Fowler, told the Parliament in her first speech that nearly ten thousand refugees were resettled in the Fairfield area between 2012 and 2017, more than anywhere else in Australia. hcIfU is a food-access initiative that has not yet served its first meal. The people I am building it for got here because somebody's war pushed them out of their own country. I did not read about that. I live among them.

You are the ones who would be sent. You are the ones who should be asked.

Sign with the Army Corps Apprentice, and ask your own government for the treaty before November.

You do not sign with me and you do not have to write to me or wait on me. The apprentice takes your name itself, keeps the least it can, shows it only if you say it may be shown, and forgets you the day you ask it to.

Until we meet again. Kia kaha. More love to you.
